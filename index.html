On Education and the Future

Upon beginning college during the Covid pandemic, I observed a dramatic shift in education by the summer of 2025. It seems nearly everyone I encounter is leveraging AI for a significant portion of their academic work. When discussing education, they often joke about using ChatGPT, and the casualness of it makes me question if this is a systemic failure or simply an exposure of the educational system's inherent inefficiencies. Any educated individual can attest to the unnecessary hurdles and "BS" encountered on their path. Why, for instance, is memorizing, by heart, L'Hopital's Rule necessary? Or why must I take a flute class requiring the purchase of a $120 personalized flute from a local artisan in Corvallis, Oregon? We're taking our brightest minds and forcing them into a rigid educational rubric, all while extracting tens of thousands of dollars. It's a shame; so much ambition and positivity could be cultivated, yet we're confined to classrooms that disregard our true aspirations.

I believe AI will usher in a future of personalized and free education. As data centers expand, robotics advance, and LLMs become increasingly accessible, humans will finally have the freedom to learn what they desire with maximum efficiency, driven by personal choice. This represents a significant departure from our current system. A free and flexible educational system is a tremendous step forward, though it undeniably presents some uncertainties.

What will the future of education look like, from preschool onward, when AGI becomes a reality? Creative endeavors will likely be at the forefront of education. The unique creativity that distinguishes humans from any present LLM will be amplified. The "idea" will be the most valuable currency. People will collaborate with AI to bring these ideas to fruition, an incredibly easy process. AI will function almost like a factory, transforming human ideas into reality. Every human will become an entrepreneur or artist of some kind. This will take a transformation of the entire educational system. We will need to go from a system that seems like it is based on memorization and change it to one that completely focuses on the production of ideas. This battery between humans and AI will have profound impacts on civilization. The genius idea to have two super powers meet and collaborate. The computer and human ideas will be profound.

What if AI turns out to be much more powerful than some anticipate? This could end up dystopic with many experts saying there is a non zero chance that AI will destroy the world. In that case we will not need an educational system. If that doesn't happen and AI is able to replicate human idea creation then the role of humans is going to be bizarre. The only things that will truly remain for humans, that I can think of, is child rearing, sport and art. Politics may still be a suitable occupation although there are many great debates on whether AI would be able to lead better than humans. These three (and a half) things will be at the center of education.

These ideas make you think about what our meaning will be in the future. When humans are subject to being inferior what truly stands out about us? Sometimes I wonder if this angst that almost all humans have is a process of socialization. We must work, make money, have a family, buy a house, etc. It feels very pressurized. Like someone is breathing down your neck. If we taught our children in a different way will the burden of this angst be lifted away? If this is indeed the case then the next generations can finally focus on what makes them human.

AI and the Critic

May 7, 2026

There are many ideas I leave unfinished in my short essays. I like to use the thoughts page to just jot down ideas that I may have. This creates an environment where I can think outside the box and try to focus on the extremes and crazy ideas without thinking about the details too much. Informal, fluid, and unorthodox.

I recently was using Google's new AI products and had it create a video for me that showed a beautiful woman playing a piano. This was the first time that AI has ever really grasped my attention from a video. The piano was beautiful, the song was crisp, and the movements were elegant. I sat there and watched the 6 second video probably 20 times while thinking about where this could go.

I try to explain how I think content will be consumed in the future by saying that we are currently creating things by pulling them from some idea space, maybe collaborating with other people, waiting for some inspiration, and working a piece over time to be consumed by others or for the feeling of self expression and uniqueness. I go on to say that in my own experience, when I am making something, I try a bunch of random things. For example, if I am painting I will mutilate a canvas until it reaches something that seems like it's at a stopping point. Or if I'm making a song I will layer different sounds to create a new sound that is just right for the vision I have or the memory I am creating in my mind. I am actively playing the role of a critic, using my body as the tool and my mind as the judge.

When I think of AI and how it created the video from the prompt, I noticed there was a similar process to how I shape my ideas when I create. I was judging the final output of the video until it reached a state of completion in my own mind. Completely customized. The insanity lies in scale. I picture someone in the future creating a painting that they really like. They worked very hard on it, and it is at the point where they say that it is theirs. Or they see another piece of work and are moved by it. Possibly, they will be able to upload data on that painting. Prompts, colors, sounds, and it can create more of it in that style in different mediums, and the artist will be able to mold it to be what they want it to be. You will basically have extremely customizable content at little cost, so everyone will be critiquing what they consume. Imagine you create a video but there's a blue cup in the background and you want it to be red. Now imagine that down to every pixel if you wanted to. It will be more of a minimizing act rather than a creating act due to excess information, although in the end it should reach the same state, assuming you have the general vision or feeling you want to express in your mind. There will naturally be some kind of marketplace where people share their work, and some creators' work will outperform others. This will create a new medium that is much richer than our current one, because cost will be low and competition and creative freedom will be high.

Now some people will say this is a disgrace and that AI can't make things that humans enjoy or can connect with, but I disagree. Right now it seems like it can't, but the human touch has not been deployed due to a lack of compute. Right now the cost of one 10 second video is between $0.50 and $5.00. There are still very interesting things being made, but they are mostly memes and ads because that is what fits into this time frame. Once humans have access to more compute, we will be able to have higher iterations of ideas at fractions of the cost. The future will be determined by the cost per token and the critic.

I can't stress enough the feeling of self expression, the vision, and having it more closely approximate this for more people through the process of critiquing. As compute scales, I think this will become more and more apparent to humans.

To conclude, I think the general way that humans create will still be viable. I just think there will be much more interesting content made in this way, and this will put pressure on the old ways of doing things, much like CGI did to the film industry. It will be beautiful.

How to Use AI

May 17, 2026

There are many ideas I leave unfinished in my short essays. I like to use the thoughts page to just jot down ideas that I may have. This creates an environment where I can think outside the box and try to focus on the extremes and crazy ideas without thinking about the details too much. Informal, fluid, and unorthodox.

What does it mean to be a specialist in AI? Everyone keeps saying "learn AI, it's the future", "You need to know how to use AI to have a job in the future" but what exactly does that mean? In this short essay I will talk about what it means to me to leverage AI to become a more productive human.

The STEM Advantage

My background is in Mechanical Engineering. Obviously this is deeply rooted in STEM and I have noticed with the rise in AI I have a critical advantage over others in how I approach AI. I think this advantage is even greater if you are specifically in Computer Science, although you'd be astonished at how many people I talk to that are studying Computer Science that are not bullish on their AI abilities.

The STEM advantage doesn't necessarily come from having a deep knowledge of mathematics or science but from a way that those things make you think about problem solving. I say this because I have seen other people have the same thought process without being in STEM. It's really the clear thinking process of how do I get from A to B as efficiently as possible. How do I manage all of the details and focus what is really the desired outcome. When studying engineering this is all we do. Dry equations and processes make the world look like it's in a state of constant inefficiency because we are looking at the theoretical values of what the ideal condition could be. For me this constantly looks like interpreting data and visual representations of data to make clear, informed decisions. This plays into how I look at using AI because this is what it is inherently very strong at. It makes the barrier of entry to interpreting very large amounts of data into something where you could possibly make a better informed decision. As long as you keep in mind what your problem is and have a defined point where it is solved, AI is an extremely powerful tool.

Using AI

I am going to talk about how I would approach a problem with the former paragraphs information in mind and it will directly show how the STEM advantage and the use of AI combine to make a better problem solver. Then talk about the future of problem solving.

Let's choose an arbitrary problem like increasing the efficiency of a vacuum cleaner. I would first look at what the properties and objectives of a vacuum cleaner are. A vacuum cleaner creates a vacuum that moves mass from an undesirable location to a desirable location, okay this can be represented as some mathematical formula from mass flow rate, power consumption and volume. What are the objectives? To create a vacuum that will take mass (dust and dirt) from one place that is not desired to another that is. Taking this information I could use some equations to describe these relationships and find an ideal state of a vacuum as the main objective. Maybe give a percentage buffer on what I am willing to accept away from that theoretical objective. Possibly scrape data from tests done on high performance or cost effective vacuums that have already been made. Then I could take all of this data and visually represent it through graphs and charts so I can make a better informed decision. We now have our base on our new product and can continue the problem solving process until we have our desired outcome.

The key here is breaking down the problem into its main parts, having clearly defined goals and having a transparent beginning and end goal then using AI to minimize the friction between all of the steps in between. AI could be used at pretty much every step besides the start and the finish.

When looking at it through this lens it appears that it isn't much different as other technological waves. We are still solving a problem and humans still need to know the first question to ask and when the desired outcome is completed. I wasn't thinking about looking up data on the internet on how to create a vacuum 100 years ago but 10 years ago you would be dumb not to do this because it would decrease the amount of time you can complete the project. I believe it's the same thing but with AI this time.

In the future by far the most important realization that will come from using AI and getting objectives done faster is that there is a quality to idea creation. It's not only about knowing how to get from A to B but what exactly are A and B and are they going to benefit my cause the greatest. I think this thought process will dominate everything else in problem solving in the next ten years and beyond. To become good at this process I think you need to understand resource allocation and human desire at a very deep level because this is what has been pushing the economic engine for the last 250 years. It's important now but this will be far more important than traditional problem solving in the future.

What Not to Do When Using AI

I recently had a buddy asking AI for relationship advice. It may have some good answers that might make you feel better but this is way too abstract of a concept for current AI capabilities. Plus, there is no clearly defined desired outcome.

All jokes aside, using AI as the crutch for your critical thinking is a very bad idea since it will hallucinate (give wrong answers). If you have no defined desired outcome then you are really just shooting in the dark which isn't a very great plan when dealing with your actual productivity and the well being of others.

This is why I don't think statements like "humans will be useless in the future" or "I'm going to graduate from college and be a prompt engineer" are valid statements. AI brings people that have worked hard on critical thinking and specializations more fruits of their labor. There is still no way to short cut the critical thinking.

Things to Think About

When using AI, especially one that doesn't have memory of past chats, it's important to think about what the perspective of the AI is. What are the inputs that it is seeing? If it were a person would it understand what the objective is? I often find myself giving short prompts and getting a non-desirable outcome because I'm being lazy and not clearly defining what I want. I think most people would say that the AI is just not very good yet but in my experience the AI is right a lot of the time and I am missing critical pieces of information. So stepping back from the problem and going back to the basics of problem solving can really help here. Making sure that the AI is interpreting your problem in the same way you are is very important. Having a dialogue and asking it if it needs clarifying questions. For me this has resulted in better productivity and reaching my desired outcomes faster.

It's also worth talking about what kinds of inputs are more robust than others. AI doesn't interpret information in the same way as us. It thinks in files and symbols. Learning which ones of these produce the least amount of friction towards your desired outcome will vastly improve your experience in using AI. As a small example of this, I could use the English language to describe what I need done in QuickBooks and this will take a relatively long time and there will likely be some kind of issue because it's not understanding me correctly. But if I upload a .csv file along with a little bit of English there is a greater chance it will give me a better answer faster that is more aligned with my desired outcome. Practicing with AI to find what inputs are going to make you reach your desired outcomes faster is critical.

Conclusion

I talk to a lot of people and there is a general worry that AI is bad. I'm not saying it is not, but I think there is a far greater chance that this new technology is much like every other technology that has come before it. It comes, the general population is scared because it is foreign (companies also make it sound scary for marketing), there are wild assumptions about it, efficient problem solvers use it to get more done and make more profits, and it slowly diffuses into the world because it is a superior way of making more wealth for people and the world. This process has been happening for thousands of years and in my personal position I think this is just the next step in that evolution.

Basketball Shot Tracker

Project Overview

ShotTracker Pro is a desktop application I built in Python for logging and analyzing pickup basketball games. The app renders a top-down court, captures every shot attempt by click position, applies live scoring rules (including a custom 19.9 ft two-point line), and persists each game to disk as a CSV. After several games have been played, a built-in analysis view pulls the full history and generates comparative plots for winners vs. losers.

Technical Stack

Core Libraries:

  • Tkinter — GUI and canvas rendering
  • csv / os / glob — file I/O and game history management
  • pandas — aggregation across game CSV files
  • matplotlib & seaborn — statistical plots (boxplot, stripplot)
  • math / datetime — distance calculation and unique game IDs

Key Features Implemented

1. Interactive Court Canvas

  • Top-down half-court view with two hoops and a center line
  • Arcs drawn at a 19.9 ft radius (199 px at 10 px/ft) representing the two-point line
  • Shot markers color-coded green for makes and red for misses
  • Circles for one-point attempts, squares for two-point attempts

2. Shot Detection & Scoring

  • Left click records a made shot, right click records a missed shot
  • Distance from the target hoop computed via Euclidean formula in pixel space, then converted to feet
  • Point value determined by whether the shot falls inside or outside the 19.9 ft arc
  • Spacebar toggles possession between the White and Black teams
  • Auto-detected win condition: first team to 15 with a 2-point lead

3. Data Export

  • Each game saves to its own timestamped CSV inside a dedicated BasketballDataFiles folder
  • Columns tracked: team, distance (ft), made flag, points scored, and win/loss label
  • Loss/win label is backfilled on every shot once the game ends, so historical analysis knows which attempts belonged to the winning team

4. Historical Analysis

  • The "Analyze All Games" button loads every CSV in the data folder via glob and concatenates them into a single pandas DataFrame
  • Plot 1: boxplot of shot distance for winners vs. losers with the 19.9 ft line overlaid — tests whether deeper shooters tend to win
  • Plot 2: stripplot of every attempt split by make/miss and winner/loser — shows how shot selection and efficiency relate to outcomes

How It Works

Coordinate System: The court is 940 × 500 pixels at a 10 px/ft scale. Hoops sit at fixed coordinates on the left and right ends, and every click is measured relative to whichever hoop the offensive team is shooting toward.

Game Loop: The app is event-driven rather than a traditional game loop — Tkinter bindings fire the appropriate handler for each click or key press. State is held on the ShotTracker instance, which means the UI, score, and shot log all stay in sync without any explicit redraw cycle.

Persistence Strategy: Rather than writing to a single growing log, each game gets its own CSV with a datetime-stamped filename. This keeps games cleanly separated for later analysis and avoids any locking or append-conflict issues.

Technical Challenges Solved

  • 1. Custom Two-Point Line: Most basketball analytics tools assume a standard NBA or college three-point arc. Supporting a non-standard 19.9 ft line required re-deriving the pixel radius and overlaying a matching visual arc on both hoops.
  • 2. Winner Attribution After the Fact: Because the winner is only known at the end of a game, every shot record needs to be updated retroactively with a Winner/Loser label before export. Solved by storing shots as dictionaries in memory and patching them in place once the win condition triggers.
  • 3. Graceful Analysis Dependencies: Pandas, matplotlib, and seaborn are optional at runtime. The app wraps the import in a try/except so the core tracker still works on a bare Python install — the Analyze button only appears if the analysis stack is present.
  • 4. Reliable File Paths: The data folder is resolved relative to the script's own directory using os.path.abspath(__file__) so the app works regardless of where the user launches it from.

What I Learned

  • Event-driven GUI design with Tkinter bindings and canvas primitives
  • Clean separation between data capture, persistence, and analysis layers
  • Using pandas to combine many small CSV files into a single analysis-ready DataFrame
  • Designing statistical visualizations that answer a specific question rather than just plotting everything

Future Enhancements

  • Heat map overlay showing shot density and field goal percentage by court zone
  • Per-player tracking instead of just team-level aggregation
  • Undo button for misclicks
  • Package the app with PyInstaller for one-click Mac/Windows distribution
  • Export summary stats (eFG%, points per possession) alongside the raw CSV

Conclusion

This project combined a practical interest — tracking pickup games without needing to remember the score — with a chance to practice GUI development and applied data analysis in Python. The end result is a lightweight, self-contained desktop tool that grows more useful with every game played, since the analysis view draws on the entire game history.

Economic Health Dashboard

Project Overview

A Python tool I built that pulls live U.S. economic data from the Federal Reserve's FRED API and renders it as a clean, self-contained visual dashboard. It tracks 50+ indicators grouped the way economists actually think about them — leading, coincident, and lagging — and compares the national picture against Oregon, Washington, and California side by side. Each indicator gets a 24-month trend chart and a full-history chart, and where a widely-cited reference point exists (like the Fed's 2% inflation target) the dashboard shows how far current data sits from it.

The interesting engineering wasn't the charts — it was the judgment calls: choosing which indicators matter, deciding that California couldn't substitute for Oregon despite being a larger economy, and being honest about which indicators have a defensible "ideal" and which don't rather than inventing targets.

Technical Stack

  • Python standard library only — no external dependencies
  • FRED API for macroeconomic time-series data
  • Hand-generated inline SVG for all charts (works offline, no chart library)
  • Self-contained HTML output — one file, opens in any browser

Sample Output

The version below is a static sample. The live tool pulls current data using a private FRED API key, which can't be published on a static site, so the figures shown are realistic placeholders rather than current data.

Conclusion

This project started as a way to check the state of the economy at a glance without clicking through a dozen government websites. It turned into an exercise in data-source judgment, honest presentation of contested reference points, and building a zero-dependency tool that just works when you run it.

The Global Race for Computation

As of writing this in the summer of 2025, it feels like the world is in the middle of a construction boom that nobody's really talking about. Not for housing, not for commercial real estate-but for data centers. These things are popping up everywhere. Microsoft's spending over $50 billion this year alone to build out infrastructure that can support Al growth. Amazon, Google, Meta-same story. It's like everyone woke up at once and realized the only way to survive the Al explosion is to own the digital land it lives on.

And when I say land, I mean literal land. Thousands of acres are being scooped up across the U.S., Scandinavia, Chile, you name it. These aren't some sleek buildings you see in tech company promo videos. They're massive, power-hungry, water-cooled beasts. We're talking about server factories the size of airports. Places like Iowa and northern Sweden-once considered pretty off the radar-are turning into digital oil fields. Why? Because they've got space, power, and cool climates. All things you need when you're trying to run and cool a building stuffed with GPUs burning hotter than a jet engine.

This is important. I don't think people fully get what this means. This isn't just about making sure ChatGPT doesn't go down during finals week. This is about reshaping the actual structure of the global economy. These data centers are becoming the foundation for a new kind of industrial revolution. One where human labor isn't the bottleneck-it's computing power. Every future business, government, artist, and engineer is going to be dependent on a cloud of infrastructure built right now. And it's not just about Al, it's about everything: logistics, health care, energy grids, education-name an industry and it's probably about to get eaten by a data-hungry algorithm.

There's something poetic about it too. The cloud used to sound like this vague, magical thing. But it turns out it's made of concrete, steel, and wires. You can drive by it. It needs real people to build and run it. It consumes electricity like it's candy. It's messy. It's expensive. And it's absolutely necessary if we want to keep scaling the capabilities of Al.

This is all happening really fast. And the consequences are going to be huge. The places that get ahead on this-places with smart energy policy, fast permitting, and cheap land-are going to explode economically. They're going to attract tech jobs, investment, and long-term relevance. Countries that miss out might find themselves on the wrong side of a new kind of digital divide. A few regions will control most of the compute power, and that's going to matter a lot more than oil did in the 20th century.

So yeah, this might seem like a boring construction story. But it's not. It's the building of a new nervous system for the planet. And if you're someone who cares about the future-about education, access, equity, and innovation-then you should be paying very close attention to where these data centers are getting built and who controls them.

About Me: The Journey

I was born in Redmond, Oregon, to a single mother who immigrated from Mexico. Shortly after I was born, we moved to Oregon City, Oregon, which is where I would consider my home. I moved all around the Willamette Valley, meeting all sorts of different people and living in strange places. When I entered elementary school, I had no problem making friends. I was always in advanced mathematics, and I would read and write at the top of my class.

When I reached high school, I joined the varsity football team, where I received an all-conference award as a distinguished wide receiver. I was also in the backstage crew for the drama department, making all kinds of props for the actors. When I was a junior, I joined an entrepreneur contest called Enterprise Academy, hosted by the Portland Rotary. It's a contest where about 100 students from all over the Portland metro area are put into random teams and have to come up with an idea. Then, we complete a plan of how we are going to create, manufacture, and pitch the idea to actual business leaders from the Portland area. My team won first place, and I won MVP of the entire camp.

My senior year and the years following high school were completely dominated by the Covid pandemic. When I went to college, I didn't feel like I was using my time as efficiently as I could be. The classes were slow, and the professors didn't quite understand how to do the whole online thing yet. It was a disaster in my opinion and a huge waste of money and time. I decided after my freshman year that I would wait for the pandemic to end to resume my studies.

In the meantime, I started renovating houses with my uncle and saving money to start aggressively traveling. I drove across 24 states in my old Chevy S-10. I went to 17 countries around the world, including Canada, Mexico, Aruba, Colombia, Peru, Portugal, Spain, France, Belgium, the Netherlands, England, Germany, Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Japan, and Mongolia.

It was during this time period that I decided I wanted to become an engineer. I remember the exact moment it hit me. I was on the wrong side of Paris in an old hotel. While the neighbors were yelling at each other through the paper-thin walls, I remember thinking this life is just like a game. I thought about it in the same way I think about chess. There are rules you must learn, then you can start using your creativity to manipulate the game. I thought I must learn the rules of the universe so that I can then manipulate the world.

When I came back from my travels, I enrolled in classes at Portland Community College. I ended up graduating and transferring to Oregon State University, where I'm currently enrolled. I'm still learning all of the intricate rules I must play by and cannot wait to get to the point where I can finally start changing the world with my creative abilities.
